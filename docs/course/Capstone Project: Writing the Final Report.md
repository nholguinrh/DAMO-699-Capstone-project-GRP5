## ***The Abstract or Executive Summary**

***The abstract or executive summary is your entire report's first impression. Its goal is to:**

- ***Provide a concise overview of your project for decision-makers, faculty, or future readers.**

- ***Allow someone can quickly understand the purpose, methods, and outcomes without reading the report.**

- ***Highlight the value of your findings and guide the reader to decide whether they want to explore more deeply.**

***Executive summaries are often the only part busy stakeholders read in professional settings, so they must stand alone as a complete, informative, and persuasive document.**

***Despite it being at the beginning, you will write the abstract after the rest of your report is finished.**

***This is because, only once you've clarified your full problem, completed your analysis, and made recommendations will you have the insight to summarize your work effectively.**

### ***What to Include**

***Aim for 1–3 pages, depending on project complexity. Write in paragraphs, not bullet points. Use transitions to create flow between sections**

#### ***Problem & Context**

***What issue are you addressing, and why does it matter?**

***Briefly describe the industry, sector, and location. Explain the socio-economic relevance (customer churn, bottlenecks in logistics, poor forecasting). State why this problem is timely and significant for stakeholders.**

#### ***Research Question & Hypothesis**

***What was the key question driving your analysis?**

***Clearly state your research question. If applicable, mention the main hypothesis you tested. Avoid technical phrasing and make it understandable to non-specialists.**

#### ***Methods Used**

***How did you analyze the data?**

***List the key methods applied (linear regression, clustering, queuing models). Group methods into themes if applicable (statistical diagnostics, marketing analytics, operations optimization). Briefly state why these were appropriate.**

#### ***Key Findings**

***What were the most important takeaways from your analysis?**

***Highlight the most impactful findings—usually 2–3 core insights. Provide meaningful interpretation ("Customer churn is highest among those who haven't interacted with support in 3+ months."). Keep it precise and action-oriented.**

#### ***Recommendations**

***Based on your findings, what should be done?**

***Summarize your main recommendations for practice or policy. Emphasize feasibility and impact. Avoid generic advice; tailor your suggestions to the context you studied.**

#### ***Checklist for Effectiveness**

***Ask yourself the following questions:**

- ***Is this clear to someone unfamiliar with my topic?**

- ***Does it cover all the critical elements of the report?**

- ***Can it stand alone as a summary of my entire work?**

- ***Does it highlight the value and relevance of my project?**

#### ***What to Avoid**

- ***Avoid overly technical language such as formulas, deep theory, or coding details.**

- ***Don’t use excessive detail, and remember that this is a summary, not the report itself, so focus on the what and why, not every how.**

- ***Use plain English instead of jargon as much as possible. Instead of "heteroskedasticity," say "unequal variation in the data."**


### ***Sample Abstract / Executive Summary**

***The following is an example of an abstract or executive summary:**

***Show Sample**

***This capstone project investigates the issue of declining customer retention within Canada's telecommunications sector, focusing on a mid-sized national service provider. High customer churn, driven by increased competition and price sensitivity, threatens profitability and long-term customer value. This study aims to develop a predictive analytics framework to identify key churn indicators and offer data-driven recommendations to improve retention strategies.**

***The research addresses the following question: What are the most significant predictors of customer churn, and how can predictive modeling inform more effective retention strategies? Based on this, the hypothesis tested was that customer engagement metrics (e.g., service usage frequency and past complaints) are stronger predictors of churn than demographic factors.**

***To explore this question, a dataset of 10,000 anonymized customer records was obtained from the company's CRM database, covering January 2022 to December 2023. The data included demographic information, contract details, usage behavior, and customer service interactions. Data cleaning involved handling missing values, standardizing variable formats, and removing duplicates. A relational database was constructed using SQL to efficiently manage and query the data.**

***Statistical techniques included logistic regression, decision tree classification, and clustering analysis. Diagnostics confirmed acceptable levels of multicollinearity, normality, and classification accuracy. Visualizations were generated using Power BI to communicate patterns and segment-specific risks.**

***The analysis revealed that length of tenure, number of support tickets filed in the last six months, and monthly service usage patterns were the strongest predictors of churn. Demographic variables had little explanatory power. Customers with short tenure and multiple service complaints were over three times more likely to churn than others.**

***Based on these insights, the report recommends a tiered intervention strategy: (1) proactively targeting high-risk customers with loyalty incentives, (2) redesigning the support workflow to reduce repeat issues, and (3) integrating churn scores into the CRM system to trigger automated alerts. These strategies are expected to reduce churn by 10–15% over the next fiscal year.**

***This project demonstrates how advanced data analytics, applied within the full analytics lifecycle, can generate actionable insights for business problems. The findings have implications for the case company and the broader telecommunications industry, navigating retention in a competitive digital environment.**

# ***Capstone Writing Manual: Academic Style, Structure & APA Integration**

***Writing a graduate-level capstone report demands more than delivering findings, and it requires clarity, structure, academic tone, and adherence to formal documentation standards. The following guidance will help you write with professionalism and cohesion across your chapters.**


## ***The Introduction Chapter**

***This chapter sets the tone for the report.**

1. ***Use formal and focused language.**

2. ***Avoid casual phrasing, overuse of the first person, or rhetorical questions.**

3. ***State your purpose directly.**

4. ***Logically move from the general context to your specific research question.**

***Use paragraphs to build the narrative. Each paragraph should begin with a clear topic sentence and be sure to transition carefully between ideas to create flow. If referencing prior research or data to contextualize the problem, cite sources using APA 7th edition format.**

![](/shared/LCS_HTML_Templates/UNF_Template_2023/_assets/img/icon_arrow.png)

#### ***Example**

- ***Recent studies have shown that customer churn in the telecommunications sector exceeds 25% annually (Nguyen & Patel, 2021).**

- ***Use signal phrases ("According to…", "As noted by…") to integrate sources smoothly. Avoid drop-in citations that appear abruptly.**


## ***The Data Collection & Preparation Chapter**

***Use precise, descriptive language to explain your process without overstating technical complexity. When referring to a dataset, cite the source, even if it is your own or public.**

***For publicly available data, follow APA 7th format:**

***Statistics Canada. (2023). Consumer Trends Dataset, 2022 \[Data set\]. Government of Canada. [https://doi.org/xxxxx**](https://doi.org/xxxxx)

***If data is proprietary, describe it thoroughly, but state clearly that it is confidential and only accessible through agreement with the provider.**

1. ***Refer to the instrument in the appendix if it's self-collected, such as surveys.**

2. ***Avoid writing like instructions. Rather than "First we cleaned the data," write: The dataset was cleaned to remove missing and inconsistent entries, ensuring the validity of subsequent analysis.**

3. ***Avoid passive overload but balance with a formal tone.**


## ***The Statistical Analysis & Diagnostics Chapter**

***This section involves technical material but should still prioritize accessibility and clarity.**

1. ***Avoid formula-heavy language unless critical. When using statistical terminology, define or explain briefly unless the term is universally known at the graduate level.**

2. ***Use consistent verb tenses:**

   - ***Describe your process in the past tense "The model was estimated using…"**

   - ***Refer to known theory or literature in the present tense "Regression analysis is commonly used when…"**

3. ***All reported statistics such as means, t-tests, p-values, should be clearly labeled and accompanied by interpretation:**

   - ***The results indicated a significant relationship between customer complaints and churn likelihood (β = 0.74, p \< .001), suggesting that customers with recent service issues are likelier to discontinue service.**

4. ***Use APA format for statistical presentation (italicize p, M, SD, t, F).**

### ***Sample: Writing the Statistical Analysis and Diagnostics Chapter**

***The following is a sample of the statistical analysis and diagnostic chapter of your capstone:**

***Show Sample**

***To examine the predictors of customer churn, binary logistic regression was employed using churn status (1 = churned, 0 = retained) as the dependent variable. Independent variables included subscription tenure, average weekly sessions, number of support tickets filed, and account suspension history. Before model estimation, all predictors were standardized to ensure the interpretability of coefficients.**

***Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all VIF values were below 2.0, indicating a low risk of redundancy. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test assessed goodness of fit (χ² (8) = 6.13, p = .631), suggesting the model fits the data well. The final model demonstrated statistically significant effects for support ticket volume (β = 0.51, p \< .001) and average session frequency (β = –0.35, p = .004). Subscription tenure was marginally significant (β = –0.19, p = .048), while age and gender were not.**

***These findings indicate that churn is significantly associated with both customer engagement and dissatisfaction events, confirming the hypothesis. Full model results are provided in Table 2.**

### ***Introduce Each Figure Before It Appears**

***Never place a figure or graph in your report without introducing it first. Readers should know what they'll see and why before encountering the visual.**

![](/shared/LCS_HTML_Templates/UNF_Template_2023/_assets/img/icon_arrow.png)

#### ***Example**

***To better understand engagement patterns by age group, Figure 2 presents average weekly session frequency by age cohort.**

***Avoid phrasing like, “Below is a chart of session frequency.” as this is too vague and informal.**

### ***Refer to All Figures by Number**

***APA 7 requires that all visuals be numbered sequentially (Figure 1, Figure 2, etc.). Using a parenthetical or embedded reference, you must refer to the visual by its figure number in the main text.**

![](/shared/LCS_HTML_Templates/UNF_Template_2023/_assets/img/icon_arrow.png)

#### ***Example**

***As shown in Figure 2, younger customers were significantly more active than those aged 50 and above.**

***There was a clear seasonal trend in subscription renewals (see Figure 3).**

***Never write "the graph below" or "the chart above." These are not APA-compliant references and become problematic if the report is reformatted.**

### ***Place the Figure After Its First Mention**

***Place the visual directly after the paragraph where it is introduced. Do not let multiple paragraphs separate the reference from the actual figure.**

### ***Title and Format the Figure (APA 7 Style)**

***In APA 7:**

- ***The title of the figure is placed below the figure, italicized and flush left.**

- ***The figure itself (chart, graph, image) should be centered and easy to read.**

- ***Axes must be clearly labeled and units of measurement specified.**

- ***Include a legend explaining colors or line styles if applicable.**

![](/shared/LCS_HTML_Templates/UNF_Template_2023/_assets/img/icon_arrow.png)

#### ***Example**

***(Figure in the middle of the page)**

***Figure 2**

***Average Weekly Session Frequency by Age Group**

***If the figure is adapted or created from external data, include a source in parentheses:**

***Figure 2: Average Weekly Session Frequency by Age Group (adapted from Statistics Canada, 2023)**

***If you use software like Power BI or Tableau, you do not need to cite the software; only the data source is used to create the visual.**

### ***Follow the Visual with Interpretation**

***A well-written report interprets the visual immediately after displaying it. The reader should not be left guessing what the figure means.**

![](/shared/LCS_HTML_Templates/UNF_Template_2023/_assets/img/icon_arrow.png)

#### ***Example**

***Figure 2 indicates a steep decline in engagement among users over 55. This pattern suggests that marketing and onboarding strategies may need to be tailored to support older age groups more effectively. Notably, users aged 25 to 34 had the highest average engagement.**

***Do not write, “Figure 2 shows some differences between groups.” as this provides no insight.**

### ***Keep Visuals Purposeful Not Decorative**

***Only include figures that:**

1. ***Clarify an important trend,**

2. ***Support a key argument or conclusion,**

3. ***Help the reader see something that cannot be conveyed in words alone.**

***Avoid "visual overload." Don't include a separate chart for every variable unless necessary. Group data meaningfully and curate what you visualize.**

### ***Follow Similar but Distinct Rules for Tables**

***For tables:**

1. ***APA tables have titles above the table, not below.**

2. ***Number them sequentially: Table 1, Table 2, etc.**

3. ***Align decimal points and use concise labels.**

4. ***After a table, follow up with a brief interpretation just as you would with a figure.**


#### ***Example**

***Table 1: Summary Statistics for Monthly Churn Rates**

***In-text reference: As shown in Table 1, the standard deviation of churn rates was highest in Q2, indicating seasonal volatility.**


# ***Final Sections: Findings & Discussions, Conclusions & Recommendations**

***The two final sections of the Report for you Capstone include the Findings and Discussion chapter which places emphasis on synthesis, and the Conclusion and Recommendations chapter which wraps things up and provides a path forward with action items.**


## ***Writing the Findings & Discussion Chapter**

***In this chapter, focus on synthesis, not just reporting.**

1. ***Use clear topic sentences to start paragraphs and transitions like:**

   - ***"In contrast,"**

   - ***"This suggests that,"**

   - ***"These results support the hypothesis that…"**

2. ***Move from evidence to interpretation:**

   - ***While age was not a statistically significant predictor of churn, usage frequency showed a strong inverse correlation (r = –.56, p \< .01), indicating that more active users tend to remain loyal.**

3. ***Always link findings back to the research question.**

4. ***Cite literature in comparison if relevant:**

   - ***This finding is consistent with earlier work by Ahmed and Luo (2020), who found similar behavior among digital banking clients.**

5. ***Avoid vague conclusions like "The results were interesting." Be specific and objective.**


## ***Writing the Conclusion & Recommendations Chapter**

***Do not simply repeat previous content. Rearticulate your main insights clearly, summarizing your argument and value-added.**

***Recommendations should be framed as responses to findings, not as generic ideas. Write in a way that implies actionability:**

***Based on the analysis, it is recommended that the firm implement a customer feedback loop within two weeks of service complaints to address early churn indicators.**

***Avoid imperatives ("The company must…"); instead, use measured, professional language: "It is recommended that…" or "Findings suggest that…"**

***Effective academic writing in a capstone report is not about sounding complicated; it's about communicating clearly and professionally. Following are essential writing style tips to help refine your report to be logical, readable, and aligned with graduate-level expectations.**

- ***Use Active Voice Where Appropriate,**

- ***Choose Clarity over Complexity,**

- ***Avoid Rhetorical Fillers & Unnecessary Phrases,**

- ***Revise for Flow, Transitions & Redundancy.**

### ***Use Active Voice Where Appropriate**

***Use active voice where it is appropriate but remain formal in your writing. The active voice strengthens your writing by making it direct and concise. In active constructions, the sentence's subject acts, rather than being acted upon.**

#### ***Preferred Active**

- ***This chapter presents the key findings of the regression analysis.**

- ***The team analyzed the cleaned dataset using logistic regression.**

#### ***Less Effective Passive**

- ***The chapter presents the key findings of the regression analysis.**

- ***The cleaned dataset was analyzed by the team using logistic regression.**


***Active voice helps your writing feel confident and professional, but do not confuse active voice with a casual tone. Keep your language formal, even when sentences are straightforward.**

### ***Choose Clarity over Complexity**

***Clarity is the cornerstone of strong academic writing. Overly complex sentences, especially those filled with jargon or excessive clauses, make your argument hard to follow.**

#### ***Clear & Professional**

***Customer engagement was measured by weekly login frequency and the number of completed sessions. These variables were selected based on their relevance to retention patterns.**

#### ***Wordy & Confusing**

***The engagement levels of customers, which are an important factor when analyzing retention and forecasting future subscription behavior, were captured using metrics such as frequency of logins per week and the number of completed sessions across the platform.**


***Aim for one main idea per sentence. Use short paragraphs to separate distinct thoughts and connect them with smooth transitions.**

### ***Avoid Rhetorical Fillers & Unnecessary Phrases**

***Phrases like "It is important to note that", "The fact that", or "Needless to say" rarely add value and often interrupt flow. These expressions can be cut without changing the meaning.**

#### ***Wordy Filler**

***The findings suggest a possible correlation.**

#### ***Improved Version**

***The findings suggest a possible correlation.**


***Some transitional or emphasis phrases can be helpful if used intentionally and sparingly to highlight key ideas.**

### ***Revise for Flow, Transitions & Redundancy**

***Strong writing develops through revisiting and revising. After drafting each section, read it aloud or review it after a short break. This helps you spot:**

- ***Abrupt transitions between sections.**

- ***Repeated ideas or duplicated explanations.**

- ***Sections that lack flow or drift off-topic.**

***Example of improving flow:**

- ***The cleaned dataset was imported into Power BI for visualization. These visualizations allowed the team to identify patterns not immediately apparent in the raw statistics. Figure 4 illustrates this by showing how login frequency varied by user segment.**

***In contrast, a poorly transitioned paragraph might jump from tool usage to findings with no bridge:**

- ***The cleaned dataset was imported into Power BI. Weekly usage was low for older users.**

***In revision, look for opportunities to guide the reader: use linking phrases like "To build on this…", "As shown in the figure below…", or "This finding aligns with the hypothesis…"**

### ***Additional Style Reminders**

1. ***Use discipline-appropriate vocabulary, but define terms where needed.**

   - ***"Multicollinearity refers to…")**

2. ***Avoid first-person unless explicitly allowed.**

   - ***Prefer "The project used three methods" over "We used three methods."**

3. ***Write in the past tense when describing what you did, and present tense when referencing general knowledge or cited literature.**

   - ***The model was estimated using R.**

   - ***Logistic regression is often used to predict binary outcomes (Hosmer et al., 2013).**

4. ***Maintain tone consistency. Don't switch between overly casual and overly rigid. Aim for professionalism, confidence, and neutrality.**
